Where Did They Learn That?

Before class begins, Maya, Jordan, and Aaliyah are talking about the week’s reading on stereotypes, prejudice, and discrimination.

Jordan says, “I observed something at my practicum site that I can’t stop thinking about. During block play, one child told another child, ‘You can’t be the builder. Girls don’t build houses.’”

Maya frowns. “What did the teacher do?”

“She stepped in gently,” Jordan says. “She said, ‘Lots of people can be builders. Let’s make sure everyone gets a chance to play.’ It was handled calmly, but it bothered me. These children are so young. Where did that idea even come from?”

Aaliyah responds, “That’s part of the point. Children are already learning messages from adults, media, books, toys, family, peers, and the larger culture. Some of those messages become stereotypes.”

Maya adds, “And if stereotypes are repeated often enough, they can affect how children treat each other.”

Jordan nods. “So prejudice and discrimination do not suddenly appear when people are older. The early messages matter.”

“Exactly,” Aaliyah says. “That is why teachers need to pay attention to what children see and hear in the classroom. Silence can allow unfair ideas to keep growing.”

Maya looks back at the chapter title. “Maybe the teacher’s job is not just to stop hurtful behavior after it happens. It is also to help children learn more respectful ways to think about themselves and others.”
