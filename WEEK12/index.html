Is That for Boys or Girls?

Before class begins, Maya, Jordan, and Aaliyah are talking about the week’s reading on gender in young children.

Jordan says, “I know children notice gender, but sometimes I wonder how much teachers should get involved. If a child says, ‘That’s a boy toy’ or ‘Girls can’t do that,’ is it really such a big deal?”

Aaliyah responds, “It can be. Young children are learning what gender means from adults, other children, books, toys, clothing, media, and classroom routines. Those messages can shape what children believe they are allowed to like, do, or become.”

Maya thinks about a preschool classroom. “So if only boys are encouraged to build, climb, or be loud, and only girls are praised for being quiet, helpful, or pretty, children may start to believe those limits are natural.”

Jordan nods. “I guess even small comments can send a message.”

“Exactly,” Aaliyah says. “Teachers do not have to lecture children. But they can gently challenge stereotypes and make sure every child has room to explore interests, feelings, friendships, and abilities.”

Maya looks back at the chapter title. “Maybe supporting children’s development means helping them see that gender should not limit who they can be.”

Think first: How can early childhood educators respond to gender stereotypes in ways that are developmentally appropriate, respectful, and supportive of all children?
