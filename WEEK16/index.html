What Do We Do With What We Have Learned?

Before class begins, Maya, Jordan, and Aaliyah are talking about the final reading for the course: systemic and internalized privilege and oppression.

Jordan looks at the chapter title and says, “This feels like the heaviest topic of the semester. I understand personal bias better now, but systemic oppression sounds bigger than anything one teacher can fix.”

Aaliyah nods. “It is bigger than one teacher. But I don’t think that means teachers are powerless. Systems are made of policies, routines, expectations, habits, and everyday decisions. Teachers participate in those systems.”

Maya adds, “And privilege does not always mean someone had an easy life. It can mean that certain parts of a system worked more smoothly for them than for others.”

Jordan thinks for a moment. “So the point is not to feel guilty or hopeless. It is to notice how power and privilege operate, and then ask what we can do differently.”

Aaliyah smiles. “That feels like one of my biggest take-aways from this class. I learned that equity is not just a word. It has to show up in materials, language, family partnerships, conflict resolution, and how we respond to children.”

Maya nods. “I learned that discomfort does not mean I should shut down. It can be a signal to listen more carefully.”

Jordan says, “And I learned that being a good teacher is not just about being kind. Kindness matters, but I also need self-awareness, humility, and the courage to question assumptions.”

Maya looks at both of them. “I think I learned from this class — and from both of you — that becoming an equity-minded teacher is not something we finish. It is something we keep practicing.”
